Doxography, the practice of compiling the "opinions" (*doxa*) of preceding philosophers, is the bedrock of our understanding of ancient thought, from the Presocratics through the Hellenistic schools.

Doxography—the systematic collection of the "opinions" (*doxa*) of philosophers—is the primary method by which the ideas of the Presocratics and other early thinkers were preserved. The tradition was pioneered by **Aristotle**, who made it a standard practice to review the theories of his predecessors before presenting his own, and **Theophrastus**, whose lost *Physicorum Opiniones* (Opinions of the Physicists) became the source for nearly all later doxographical manuals.

### The Aristotelian Foundation
Aristotle viewed the history of philosophy as a teleological process leading toward his own system. In his *Metaphysics*, he explicitly states that the study of causes requires a knowledge of "the opinions which the ancients entertained respecting them." — *[The Metaphysics of Aristotle](https://sourcelibrary.org/book/the-metaphysics-of-aristotle-taylor)*, [Page 31](https://sourcelibrary.org/book/the-metaphysics-of-aristotle-taylor/page-number/31).
> "But, since the speculation of causes requires a previous knowledge of their first genera, and of the opinions which the antients entertained respecting them, hence, in the first book, Aristotle relates what the antients knew of the first genera of causes..."
— *[The Metaphysics of Aristotle](https://sourcelibrary.org/book/the-metaphysics-of-aristotle-taylor)* by [Aristotle](https://sourcelibrary.org/author/aristotle), [Page 31](https://sourcelibrary.org/book/the-metaphysics-of-aristotle-taylor?page=31)

### Major Doxographical Works
The library holds several key texts that exemplify this tradition, ranging from classical antiquity to the Renaissance:

*   **Diogenes Laertius**: His *Lives and Opinions of Eminent Philosophers* is the most famous surviving example of the genre, blending biographical anecdotes with summaries of philosophical doctrines. We hold several significant editions, including a [1533 Greek edition](https://sourcelibrary.org/book/de-vitis-dogmatibus-et-apophthegmatibus-philosophorum-laertius) and a [1472 Latin translation](https://sourcelibrary.org/book/vitae-et-sententiae-philosophorum-tr-ambrosius-tra-diogenes-laertius) by Ambrogio Traversari.
*   **The Placita Philosophorum**: Often attributed to **Plutarch** or **Aetius**, this work organizes the opinions of philosophers by topic (e.g., the nature of the sun, the cause of eclipses) rather than by author. A 15th-century manuscript in our collection preserves these accounts, such as **Xenophanes'** theory that an eclipse is a "quenching" of solar fire.
    > "Xenophanes; a philosopher who often explained celestial phenomena as being made of clouds [says an eclipse occurs by] a quenching... an extinction or 'putting out' of the solar fire."
    — *[Works of Aristotle, Ptolemy, and Hero of Alexandria](https://sourcelibrary.org/book/bodleian-library-ms-barocci-70-alexandria)* by [Aristotle](https://sourcelibrary.org/author/aristotle), [Page 371](https://sourcelibrary.org/book/bodleian-library-ms-barocci-70-alexandria?page=371)
*   **Hermann Diels**: For the modern scholar, the definitive reconstruction of these sources is found in *Doxographi Graeci* (1879), which we hold in its [original Greek/Latin edition](https://sourcelibrary.org/book/the-greek-doxographers-diels).

### The "Turba Philosophorum"
In the alchemical tradition, doxography took a unique turn with the *Turba Philosophorum* (The Assembly of the Philosophers). This text presents a fictionalized debate where ancient Greek philosophers discuss the principles of alchemy, effectively creating an "alchemical doxography."
> "...hence the book in which the opinions of many regarding χρυσοποιεία gold-making are collected is inscribed Turba philosophorum (The Crowd of Philosophers)."
— *[On the Agreement of the Chymists with the Aristotelians and Galenists](https://sourcelibrary.org/book/de-chymicorum-cum-aristotelicis-et-galenicis-consensu-sennert)* by Daniel Sennert, [Page 39](https://sourcelibrary.org/book/de-chymicorum-cum-aristotelicis-et-galenicis-consensu-sennert?page=39)

You might explore how **Theophrastus** structured his survey of natural philosophy, or examine the **Turba Philosophorum** to see how alchemists co-opted the names of ancient sages.

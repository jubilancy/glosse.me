“The “drunk” hypothesis, recently elaborated by Slingerland (2021), argues that the benefits of alcohol consumption far outweigh its many harmful health (Zhao et al. 2023), reproductive (Van Heertum and Rossi 2017), and social (Klingemann and Gmel 2001) consequences. Specifically, Slingerland argues that intoxication facilities life in an unusual ecological niche to which humans have adapted − one that requires culture to thrive and survive. Whether it is tools, clothing, norms or institutions, people in all societies, even the most low-tech ones, depend on cultural technologies and innovations. But these solutions to life’s challenges are beyond the capabilities of any single individual. Instead, they require a “collective brain” and cultural transmission, which in turn cannot happen without large-scale social cooperation. And this is where the role of alcohol drinking comes in. As intoxication reduces cognitive control, it may enhance lateral thinking and lead to more creative insights (Jarosz et al. 2012), as well as more openness to bond with (Sayette et al. 2012), cooperate with and learn from others. In addition, some scholars (Katz and Voigt 1986; Smalley and Blake 2003) argue that it was primarily the desire for alcohol (not food) that motivated hunter-gatherers to begin cultivating crops, settle down, and live in large, dense groups. Getting drunk may have subsequently helped to alleviate stress (Levenson et al. 1980; Peele and Brodsky 2000) that could easily arise from this dramatic change and life in cramped, hierarchical societies. Alcohol may therefore have been essential for the rise and maintenance of human civilization.”
